Notes that turn into work.

ioths keeps the task inside the note. Add a checklist and the note appears on a kanban board built for one person, with a limit you set on how much can be in progress. Every note is a plain Markdown file on your device, in the same format Obsidian uses.

01 / What’s different

Four choices, made on purpose.

What ioths does differently.

The task lives in the note.

Add a checklist to any note and it becomes a task note. Its card shows how far along it is, and the Tasks screen gathers every open item from across your notes.

A kanban board for one.

Open and done follow your checklists; you flag what you are working on. A work-in-progress limit that you set stops you starting more than you meant to carry.

Plain files, in storage you choose.

Every note is a Markdown file with frontmatter Obsidian understands. Keep them on the device, or use a Files folder, GitHub or GitLab.

Private, offline, no account.

Everything works without a network and without an ioths account. Analytics are off unless you turn them on, and nothing is sold or used to train a model.

03 / About

Why it exists.

Why I made it.

I built ioths for myself because the notes apps I had tried never quite fitted, either in what they did or how they felt to use.

The result is fairly specific: somewhere to write on the phone, keep the file, and add tasks without copying the thought into another tool. If you already use Obsidian, the file format will be familiar.

04 / The icon

What it refers to.

A note, dissolving into cells.

The icon nods to Conway’s Game of Life: a few fixed rules, and a pattern whose course you cannot read from its first shape. A scrap of writing can turn out the same way, into a decision or a piece of work.

It is only a comparison. A note does not cause its own next step; new circumstances and attention do.

The rules, in full

Conway’s Game of Life is played on a grid of square cells. Each cell is either alive or empty. To find the next generation, count each cell’s neighbours: the eight cells that touch it, diagonals included. Then apply four rules.

  1. Loneliness. A living cell with fewer than two living neighbours dies.
  2. Survival. A living cell with two or three living neighbours stays alive.
  3. Crowding. A living cell with more than three living neighbours dies.
  4. Birth. An empty cell with exactly three living neighbours becomes alive.

Every cell is judged at the same moment, using only the current generation. The whole grid then changes at once. The mathematician John Conway devised the rules in 1970.

The field here is finite: the left and right edges join, as do the top and bottom, so a pattern that leaves one side returns from the other. It starts from the R-pentomino, five cells that take 1,103 generations to settle on an unbounded grid. On a small, wrapped field they unfold differently, so use Step to follow the first few generations.
